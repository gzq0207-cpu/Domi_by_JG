【核心观点】复盘1990年代中期 DVD 标准大战：索尼/飞利浦 MMCD 阵营对东芝/华纳 SD（Super Density）阵营，几乎重演 VHS-Betamax 之战，最终在 IBM 领衔施压下调和为统一的 DVD 标准——格式大战没有赢家，握有生态话语权的第三方巨头才是事实上的裁决者。
【关键事实】CD 的暴利（制造成本约2.35美元、零售约16美元）驱动各方寻找"下一张光盘"。1994年好莱坞七大内容商提出统一光盘标准需求：单面135分钟全长电影、画质优于 LaserDisc、环绕声/多语言/多宽高比、必须有防拷贝。两大方案：MMCD（3.7GB/面、向下兼容 CD 生态、索尼+飞利浦，哥伦比亚归属使索尼利益一致）vs SD（更小坑点密排5GB/面+0.6mm薄基板双面贴合8-9GB+双层单面10GB，东芝+松下领衔、华纳带10家内容商跟随）。1995年苹果/康柏/HP/IBM/微软五巨头先劝和未果；IBM（光存储专家 Alan Bell 拉来 CEO Lou Gerstner 亲自出面）以"几周内不妥协就公开支持 SD"施压，促成8月24日和解：融合 SD 的贴合基板+纠错与 MMCD 的数据调制方案，容量从5GB微调为经典的4.7GB（IBM 表示不在乎0.3GB）。命名妥协于"DVD"。拷贝保护谈判拖延使派拉蒙/梦工厂/福克斯初期缺席；1996年11月日本首发（东芝 SD-3000），1997年3月美国上市（播放器600-1000美元、约30部影片）。起步不及预期：1997年底美国仅售34万台（预期120万）；1998年 Netflix 邮寄租赁起步、节日季走强；1999年中国产200美元播放器引爆市场；2000年 PS2 内置 DVD（至2001年3月售出千万台）；2001年美国 DVD 与 VHS 消费支出持平（各约50亿美元）。中国支线：姜万勐1993年与 C-Cube 合作在合肥造出全球第一台 VCD 播放机"万燕"（5000元/台首销罄），因整机设计未申请专利被快速抄袭、广告大战拖垮，1996年卖身美菱冰箱；但 VCD 就此成为中国主流格式（1997年销量1000-1500万台），步步高/新科等本土厂商把外国品牌挤出市场；因无防拷贝，片商内容供给少，港版盗版碟反而繁荣。

这本可能会酿成一场灾难。在mid-1990s，两个对立阵营围绕数字影碟的未来展开较量。一方是 CD 的创造者飞利浦和索尼，他们推出了用于播放电影的 CD。另一方则是东芝和华纳兄弟推出的光盘。哪一种更好？有一段时间，局面看起来像是 VHS 和 Betamax 之争重演。今天的视频将讲述 DVD 热潮戏剧性的诞生。## 加载中……在1982，索尼和飞利浦用 CD 革新了音频领域。它是首款真正被大众接受的大规模消费级数字产品。

它不仅音质出色，还能暂停、重新播放、快进、跳过曲目，以及做各种操作。而且光盘本身小巧、坚固，还装在漂亮的珠宝盒式包装里。在1980年代，CD 销售为音乐产业带来了巨额收入。更便宜的新款 CD 播放器陆续问世。唱片公司争相将热门作品重新发行到 CD 上。Sam Goody 和 Tower Records 等连锁零售商也纷纷采用这种格式。销量激增。1983，CD 售出了5.5百万张。五年后的1988，全球 CD 的制造量达到了惊人的400百万张。次年又生产了600百万张 CD。

此外，当时每张 CD 的制造成本约为$2.35，零售价却约为$16。即便扣除艺人和词曲作者的版税、营销成本以及零售商利润，仍然能赚很多钱。简直是一场盛宴！## CD-ROM 续集就像每一部爆款电影都会推出续集一样，CD 标准组织也迅速推出了后续产品。最成功的续作紧随其后，名为 CD 只读存储器，也就是 CD-ROM。想法很简单：我们能不能用 CD 存储数据库之类的原始计算机数据，而不是音频录音？

CD-ROM 于1983推出，起步缓慢——微软在1986举办的首届国际 CD-ROM 大会只吸引了一千名技术发烧友。直到1990年代，CD-ROM 进入个人电脑市场后，才真正开始大展身手。CD 的存储容量高得多，再加上声卡、彩色显示器和扬声器，让台式电脑变成了多媒体设备。微软大力支持 CD-ROM，推出了 Encarta 和 Microsoft Bookshelf 等一系列广受欢迎的软件产品。后来，其他互动多媒体产品也进一步推动了它的普及，例如电子游戏《Myst》和《7th Guest》。

CD-ROM 最终大获成功。到1993，这个行业的规模已达数十亿美元，数千人参加微软的 CD-ROM 大会。飞利浦认为 CD 格式还有更多可能性，便开始探索这些可能性。他们下一个重要项目是 CD-Interactive。在微软首届 CD-ROM 1986大会上，这一项目正式发布——微软自己可能也颇感意外——CD-I 是一个结合了光盘存储与互动内容的硬件平台。我马上想到电子游戏——我想飞利浦后来确实尝试过——但它最初

它最初的定位是“寓教于乐”。&nbsp;博物馆导览、家庭娱乐之类的内容，以及销售点。&nbsp;&nbsp;公司在接下来的几年里努力推广 CD-I，但收效甚微。## 接下来值得关注什么？一个显而易见的目标是好莱坞和家庭视频。基于光盘的家庭观影技术自 1970年代以来就已存在。&nbsp;&nbsp;著名的例子包括 LaserDisc——一种由 Philips 和 MCA 最初开发、由 Pioneer 大力推广的高分辨率格式。它在爱好者中取得了小众成功。但最终席卷市场的却是基于盒式磁带的 VHS 格式，它始于……

20 世纪 1970年代后期。便宜的 VHS 磁带为制片厂创造了一种全新的媒介，用来销售他们的内容。VHS 还催生了另一项重要的收入来源：录像带租赁店。制片厂以加价后的批发价向这些店出售录像带——每盘约 60 美元至 100 美元——店主随后通过出租录像带逐渐收回成本。（后来，制片厂将这种模式改为收入分成。）到 1988 年，美国录像带租赁市场规模已达 5.5 十亿美元，全国各地有数千家租赁店。总体而言，家庭录像观看为制片厂贡献了超过 50% 的收入。

然而，到1980年代末，VHS 市场的增长开始趋于停滞，VCR 和录像带租赁店的发展也不例外。与此同时，有线电视和卫星电视的竞争仍持续给制片厂带来压力。下一步该怎么办？各家公司尝试推广新格式。1987，JVC 推出了 Super-VHS，这是一种向后兼容、分辨率更高的格式。录像带与 VHS 相同，但里面的磁带质量更高。据称画面晶莹清晰、毫无瑕疵。Super-VHS 格式与8毫米磁带展开竞争。索尼则支持一种名为Video8的格式，

这种格式后来发展成了Hi-8。虽然8毫米磁带未能打入家庭视频市场，但它们体积更小，因此成为这些如今已是古董的设备——摄像机——的标准。对于不明白的人来说，摄像机是20世纪的单用途录像设备。它们不像手机那样能打电话或上网，只能把视频录到磁带上。总之，VHS 看起来无可匹敌。它的生态系统已经根深蒂固。VHS 录像带和播放器价格低廉，在 CRT 电视上的画质也足够好。全球有189百万户家庭拥有 VCR。

客户使用录像机（VCR）不只是为了家庭影院，也会录下电视节目留待以后观看。 ## CD Video CD 首次大举进军视频领域时，采用的是一种名为 CD Video 的格式。CD Video 于 1987 推出，将 CD 的数字音效与模拟视频轨道结合起来。普通 CD 播放机可以播放 CD-V 的音频，但要在电视上播放视频，则需要专用播放器。它推出了几种光盘尺寸，最小的一种与标准 CD 一样大。但由于视频以模拟 FM 信号而非数字信号的形式存储在光盘上，

较小尺寸的光盘只能存储约五到六分钟的视频，以及 20 分钟的音频。它的卖点是让观众可以观看音乐视频。和 CD 一样，最初的 CD-V 收录的是古典音乐和歌剧，但用户体验很奇怪：五分钟的视频之后，是 20 分钟没有画面、只有声音的空白。开发这种格式期间，飞利浦曾向华纳兄弟的一位高管演示这项技术，此人名叫 Warren Lieberfarb，当时担任华纳家庭录像部门总裁。Lieberfarb 日益担忧来自数字卫星和有线电视的竞争。

频道越多，看有线电视的人就越多，而不是去当地的录像带出租店。他得出结论，解决办法是以实惠的价格（约 10 至 12 美元）提供优于有线电视的视频和音频质量。受 CD Video 演示的启发，Lieberfarb 研究了 LaserDisc 的经济效益，并注意到大批量生产时，光盘的单位制造成本可能只有 VHS 录像带的一半。从那时起，他便把将电影存储到光盘基片上作为自己的使命。

Lieberfarb 力促华纳兄弟与飞利浦建立合作关系，共同开发一种新标准，提供 CD 级音质和至少达到 VHS 水平的视频质量。不幸的是，早期围绕 MPEG-1 压缩编解码器开展的工作，结果不太理想。一年后，华纳宣布与时代公司合并，成立当时全球最大的媒体公司：时代华纳。随后在 1991，日本公司东芝和伊藤忠商事以 10 亿美元收购了时代华纳 12.5% 的股份。两家公司有意探索将自身的硬件经验与华纳的媒体和有线电视资产结合起来的机会。

华纳也需要这笔钱来偿还债务。华纳什么时候不背着一大笔债？飞利浦与华纳的合作进展甚微，Lieberfarb 便与东芝会面，了解他们在数字压缩方面取得了什么进展。他看到的成果好得多，于是华纳在 1992 转而与东芝合作。不过一年后，华纳似乎又与飞利浦和解，并签署了一份意向书。 ## Video CD 飞利浦继续开发 MPEG-1 编解码器。

他们最初试图将其整合进 CD-I 平台，并与派拉蒙影业达成一项 1993 协议，由后者为 CD-I 提供约 50 部电影。不幸的是，这仍不足以促使足够多的人购买硬件。不过，这项工作最终促成了一种全新、独立的格式，名为 Video CD。不，这和 CD Video 不是一回事。是 VCD，不是 CD-V。总之，得益于 MPEG-1 压缩，VCD 能存储比 CD-V 更多的内容——约 74 分钟，而不是五分钟。不幸的是，它与 VHS 录像带竞争时仍处于劣势。

VCD 播放机比录像机贵，而且不能录制电视节目。更令人失望的是，约 240p 的压缩图像分辨率大致只与 VHS 相当。那又何必呢？在西方，VCD 被批评为多余又昂贵。然而，这种格式在亚洲华语地区迅速走红，这在很大程度上要归功于姜万勐的努力。1992，姜万勐在美国的一场国际电视技术展览会上接触到了 MPEG-1。姜万勐与这家 MPEG 公司——名为 C-Cube——合作，在合肥生产出全球第一台 VCD 播放机。

这台播放机名为“万燕”。尽管每台售价高达 5,000 元人民币（867 美元），万燕的首批产品还是售罄了。VHS 尚未在中国大陆站稳脚跟，因此万燕是当时家用视频市场上唯一的选择。不幸的是，万燕招来了竞争者，他们照抄了这款设备的设计——该设计没有申请专利——还在广告上大手笔投入。激烈竞争最终迫使万燕于 1996 出售给美菱冰箱公司。但这股热潮让 VCD 成为中国大陆确立地位的标准，1997 年售出 10-15 百万台播放机。

最终，步步高、爱多和新科等本土公司也推出了自己的 VCD 播放机，将外国厂商挤出了市场。VCD 作为主流格式又持续了好几年。由于这种格式没有防拷贝保护，电影公司没有为 VCD 发行多少内容。但这并未阻止人们在电影院里偷拍电影，再把影片内容刻录到 VCD 上廉价出售——尤其是在香港。 ## 发出号召 在 1994 八月下旬或九月初的某个时候，全球七家最大内容制作公司的代表齐聚一堂。

他们呼吁建立一种全球统一的光盘标准，以便用于发行电影。该标准需要满足一些技术要求：单面光盘必须能容纳一部长篇电影，也就是约 135 分钟内容。画质必须优于 LaserDisc 等现有高端视频系统。它还必须支持环绕声、多种语言和多种画面宽高比。出于显而易见的原因，它也必须具备防拷贝保护。相关经济利益十分巨大。米高梅/联美家庭娱乐总裁 Richard Cohen 对《洛杉矶时报》说：

> “如果以一种方式推出数字视频光盘方案，让大量消费者看过后都说‘这比磁带好，也比 LaserDisc 好’，那么对于我们这些掌握片库的人来说，这就成了一个面向大众市场的机会，可以把这些电影重新卖一遍。”唱片公司从 CD 中赚了数十亿美元。如果数字视频光盘格式取得成功，电影公司也有望获得同样多甚至更多的收益。这类标准的专利持有人也有望从许可费中赚取数百万美元。为满足这一要求，各种方案陆续出现。在这一番角力中，

两大阵营浮出水面，各自推出了不同但相似的方案。 ## MMCD 尽管华纳和飞利浦在1993达成了一些和解，飞利浦最终还是离开，重新加入索尼——他们在 CD 项目中的成功伙伴。199412 月，他们公开宣布了自己的下一项重大产品：多媒体 CD，简称 MMCD。它最初的名称是 HDCD，但这个名称已经被占用了。MMCD 的尺寸与音频 CD 相同，每一面都能存储多得多的数据——约3.7吉字节。这样一来，单面光盘就能容纳约135分钟的视频，勉强达到了要求。

MMCD 联盟已签下14家初始支持者，其中大多是诺基亚、Bang & Olufsen 和理光这样的硬件公司。索尼在1989收购了大型电影公司哥伦比亚影业，因此他们也站在同一阵营。 ## 超级密度 在 MMCD 公布一个月后，以东芝为代表的一群日本消费电子公司宣布了他们全新且不同的标准：“Super Disc”，也称“Super Density Disc”。光盘表面有一系列深坑和平坦区域，后者称为“平台”。读取 CD 时，激光会扫描光盘表面。当激光照射到坑上时，反射的光会比照射到平台上时少。随后，机器中的光电二极管接收器会感测反射光的变化模式，并产生可由处理器解读的数字电信号。如果我们想在光盘上存储更多数据，就需要在光盘表面排布更多的坑。

SD 采用更小、排列更紧密的坑来实现这一点——坑的尺寸约为0.4微米，间距为0.74微米；相比之下，MMCD 的坑尺寸为0.45微米，间距为0.84微米。这样一来，Super Density 光盘单面就能存储5吉字节，

The financial stakes were large.&nbsp;&nbsp; MGM/United Artists' Home Entertainment&nbsp; President Richard Cohen told the LA Times:

> "If a digital video disc solution is presented&nbsp; in a way that large numbers of consumers look at&nbsp;&nbsp; it and say 'This is better than tape and better&nbsp; than LaserDisc', then it becomes a mass product&nbsp;&nbsp; opportunity for people such as us who control&nbsp; libraries to sell the films all over again" The record labels made billions from CD.&nbsp;&nbsp; The studios stood to do just as well or better&nbsp; from a successful Digital Video Disc format. The patent-holders of such a standard also stood&nbsp; to reap millions of dollars from licensing fees. Various proposals emerged to meet&nbsp; this request. Amidst the shuffle,&nbsp;&nbsp;

two large camps emerged with&nbsp; different but similar offerings. ## MMCD While Warners and Philips&nbsp; did make some peace in 1993,&nbsp;&nbsp; Philips eventually left to rejoin Sony&nbsp; - their successful partner from the CD. In December 1994, they publicly&nbsp; announced their next big thing:&nbsp;&nbsp; Multimedia CD, or MMCD. The original&nbsp; name was HDCD but that was already taken. An MMCD was the same size of an audio CD with&nbsp; each side able to store far more data - about&nbsp;&nbsp; 3.7 gigabytes. That lets you put about 135&nbsp; minutes of video onto a single disc side&nbsp;&nbsp; which met the requirements just barely.

The MMCD alliance had signed 14 initial&nbsp; backers, mostly hardware companies like Nokia,&nbsp;&nbsp; Bang and Olufsen, and Ricoh. Sony had bought&nbsp; the major movie studio Columbia in 1989,&nbsp;&nbsp; so they too were aligned. ## Super Density A month after MMCD's announcement, a coven&nbsp; of Japanese consumer electronics companies&nbsp;&nbsp; including Toshiba announced their new, different&nbsp; standard: "Super Disc" or "Super Density Disc". The Compact Disc surface features a series&nbsp; of deep pits and flat areas called "lands".&nbsp;&nbsp; When we read the CD, a laser&nbsp; scans over the surface.&nbsp;&nbsp;

When the laser light hits a pit, it doesn't&nbsp; reflect as much as it does when hitting a land. The subsequent pattern of reflections are&nbsp; sensed by a photodiode receptor in the machine,&nbsp;&nbsp; which then produces a digital electric signal&nbsp; that can be interpreted by a processor. If we want to put more data onto the disc, we&nbsp; need to stuff more pits onto the disc surface.&nbsp;&nbsp; SD did this with smaller pits packed tighter&nbsp; together - about 0.4 microns large and 0.74&nbsp;&nbsp; microns apart compared to MMCD's 0.45&nbsp; microns large and 0.84 microns apart. This gives the Super Density disc&nbsp; 5 gigabytes of storage on one side&nbsp;&nbsp;

as compared to MMCD's 3.7 gigabytes. SD has a thinner substrate,&nbsp; about 0.6 millimeters thick.&nbsp;&nbsp; This makes it possible to bond&nbsp; two sides together back to back,&nbsp;&nbsp; creating 8-9 gigabytes of total storage across&nbsp; both sides. But you will need to flip the disc. Super Disc also offered an optional&nbsp; second approach for even more capacity:&nbsp;&nbsp; Dual layers. The first information&nbsp; layer, let's call it Layer A,&nbsp;&nbsp; is semi-transparent. The second information&nbsp; layer, Layer B, is fully reflective. Layer A being semi-transparent means&nbsp; that it can reflect just enough light&nbsp;&nbsp;

for its pits to be read by the&nbsp; laser if focused directly on it. But at the same time, the laser can&nbsp; also fire through it to focus on and&nbsp;&nbsp; read from Layer B below. I know,&nbsp; right? Doesn't that sound crazy?&nbsp;&nbsp; This makes it possible to get up&nbsp; to 10 gigabytes on a single side. The SD Alliance composed of seven companies,&nbsp; mostly Japanese consumer electronics companies,&nbsp;&nbsp; led by Toshiba and Matsushita&nbsp; or Panasonic - Sony's arch-rival&nbsp;&nbsp; and the key factor in VHS winning over Betamax. Their allies included a gang of 10 companies&nbsp; - mostly home electronics manufacturers&nbsp;&nbsp;

and the movie studios with Warner leading the way. ## Vaporware versus Patents So on one side, we had&nbsp; MMCD. On the other side, SD. The SD Gang argued that SD&nbsp; was the technically superior&nbsp;&nbsp; approach that met all the studios'&nbsp; requirements - including playtime.&nbsp;&nbsp; In September 1994, a Warners&nbsp; executive told the Los Angeles Times: > Ours has a higher data capacity. The higher&nbsp; data capacity, the longer the running time,&nbsp;&nbsp; the higher the data transfer rate, the higher&nbsp; the picture quality, the more audio channels,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the more capacity for multiple&nbsp; soundtracks and multiple subtitle tracks.

The MMCD crowd argued that their technology&nbsp; built off the successful audio CD ecosystem.&nbsp;&nbsp; An MMCD player can play existing CDs.&nbsp;&nbsp; This minimized customer confusion&nbsp; and ensured backwards compatibility. Moreover, they attacked the SD product as&nbsp; theoretical and un-manufacturable at volume.&nbsp;&nbsp; The bonding of the two half-discs had to&nbsp; be done perfectly without voids or bubbles.&nbsp;&nbsp; So in essence, they were calling it vaporware. But the SD folks had their own issues with MMCD.&nbsp; More than a few people grumbled about the rich&nbsp;&nbsp; patent fees that Philips and its partners&nbsp; were making from the audio CD bonanza.

The exact amount that Sony and Philips have&nbsp; made from audio CD royalties is not public,&nbsp;&nbsp; but contemporary estimates pegged&nbsp; it at about 3 cents for each CD.&nbsp;&nbsp; In 1993, 1.4 billion CDs were manufactured,&nbsp; yielding a nice $42 million in royalties. To the SD folks, this was a butt-naked attempt&nbsp; to bring the CD gravy train to digital video.&nbsp;&nbsp; They reached out to the US Justice Department&nbsp; - which was then already investigating Philips&nbsp;&nbsp; and Sony for anti-competitive patenting&nbsp; practices - and presented their case. ## The Path to War The CD-ROM's increasing success as&nbsp; a data storage format meant that&nbsp;&nbsp;

the computer-makers had a stake in the game too. In late April 1995, five big computer&nbsp; companies - Apple, Compaq, HP, IBM,&nbsp;&nbsp; and Microsoft - came together to&nbsp; urge the two sides to set aside&nbsp;&nbsp; their quarrels and make peace. But&nbsp; both sides refused to compromise. Norio Ohga, Sony's chairman and CEO,&nbsp; vigorously argued for their disc.&nbsp;&nbsp; He told the press in May 1995 that "a split&nbsp; on the standard is unavoidable because we are&nbsp;&nbsp; in a world of democracy". To him, this was&nbsp; a question of "liberalism and democracy". Then in a surprising twist, the MMCD&nbsp; camp announced that they would adopt&nbsp;&nbsp;

their own dual-layer technology licensed from 3M.&nbsp;&nbsp; 3M's technology stamped a second layer of&nbsp; pits onto a transparent layer on a disc. This approach differed from&nbsp; Toshiba's wafer bonding approach,&nbsp;&nbsp; but it narrowed the gap between the&nbsp; two disc formats' data capacities. Frankly, nobody wanted another VHS-Betamax&nbsp; - which was horrific for everyone involved&nbsp;&nbsp; and confused the customer to no end.&nbsp; There was so much money at stake. But both sides refused to back down. The&nbsp; LA Times in July 1995 had a wonderful set&nbsp;&nbsp; of quotes from everyone on each side.&nbsp; Philips' lead man Jan Oosterveld said:

> "If they say they want one format, then&nbsp; I would like to hear what they propose.&nbsp;&nbsp; We have invited people to join us since last year" Lieberfarb retorted: > "We've issued three invitations&nbsp; for them to talk and ... no response.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is about Sony and Philips trying to maintain&nbsp; their hegemony over optical disc technology" Sony's then-President Nobuyuki&nbsp; Idei told the press in June 1995: > "Do you want to turn over your CD? We&nbsp; have no intention to compromise on that.&nbsp;&nbsp; That would not be to the benefit of the consumer" He means literally, physically turning&nbsp; over the disc inside the player.&nbsp;&nbsp;

Toshiba's senior manager Koji Hase snapped back: > "He always says that about turning over the&nbsp; disc. It's so craftily done. But it's not true.&nbsp;&nbsp; Ours has more room on one side than theirs.&nbsp; How can we support a lesser format?" As the summer of 1995 came to a close, it became&nbsp; increasingly clear that the two sides were heading&nbsp;&nbsp; to war, err to market. MMCD said they were&nbsp; going to launch before the winter holiday.&nbsp;&nbsp; SD was insistent on launching&nbsp; mid-1996. Here we go again. ## IBM In the end, it took the computer makers - led by&nbsp; a third-party kingmaker - to break the deadlock.

In August 1995, the technical group of&nbsp; seven computer-makers issued a press&nbsp;&nbsp; release saying that both formats met all the&nbsp; requirements for next generation data storage. The only thing missing was a single,&nbsp; unified format. The computer-makers&nbsp;&nbsp; said in their press release that it will not&nbsp; back either format until they consolidated.&nbsp;&nbsp; They were obviously trying to&nbsp; force the two sides together. When that failed, IBM - led by noted optical&nbsp; disc expert Alan Bell, who brought in IBM&nbsp;&nbsp; chairman and CEO Lou Gerstner himself - went&nbsp; to Sony and Philips and told them if they did&nbsp;&nbsp;

not come to a compromise within a few weeks,&nbsp; then IBM intended to publicly come out for SD. This finally got things rolling. On August&nbsp; 24th, the two sides announced that they&nbsp;&nbsp; were meeting to hammer out a combined&nbsp; standard with aspects of both sides. The resulting format had SD's bonded&nbsp; substrates and error correction plus&nbsp;&nbsp; MMCD's data modulation scheme,&nbsp; which encodes digital 1s and 0s&nbsp;&nbsp; to the sequence of pits and lands to&nbsp; read. Kind of like a spelling system. Adopting the MMCD scheme slightly reduced disc&nbsp; capacity from 5 gigabytes to the now-famous&nbsp;&nbsp;

number of 4.7 gigabytes. SD grumbled but IBM&nbsp; said they didn't care about 0.3 gigabytes. What should they call it? The&nbsp; journalist Stewart Wolpin in&nbsp;&nbsp; a very excellent piece from 2007 - go&nbsp; read it - reported a tussle on naming.&nbsp;&nbsp; Sony and Philips wanted "CD" in&nbsp; the name so IBM suggested SMCD. The SD people were a hard NO on that.&nbsp; So they picked up a colloquial nickname&nbsp;&nbsp; that had been floating around for the&nbsp; past year, Digital Video Disc, DVD. Except DVD was supposed to do data storage&nbsp; too, so they changed the V to Versatile.&nbsp;&nbsp;

Officially DVD just means DVD. In December 1995,&nbsp; the two sides put together the DVD Consortium,&nbsp;&nbsp; which handled and guided the&nbsp; standard. Today that's the DVD forum. ## Closing the Movie Studios The final thing was getting the movie studios&nbsp; to actually release their movies on DVD. But then arose a new major&nbsp; sticking point: Copy protection.&nbsp;&nbsp; The studios worried that releasing&nbsp; DVDs would enable people to rip&nbsp;&nbsp; perfect digital copies of their movies.&nbsp; Particularly with DVD-ROM systems. Toshiba, Matsushita and others hammered&nbsp; out an acceptable copy protection scheme,&nbsp;&nbsp;

which took a substantial amount&nbsp; of time. Unsure about piracy,&nbsp;&nbsp; studios like Paramount, Dreamworks and Fox&nbsp; initially held their movies from the DVD roster. Toshiba announced that the first DVD players will&nbsp; enter the market in late 1996, launching in Japan&nbsp;&nbsp; first. In September, Toshiba announced the SD-3000&nbsp; DVD player, which began selling in November 1996.&nbsp;&nbsp; The next month, Warner Brothers released&nbsp; four of their movies on DVD in Japan. The US launch followed a few&nbsp; months later in March 1997,&nbsp;&nbsp; with at least 20 companies launching&nbsp; players priced between $600 and $1,000&nbsp;&nbsp;

and about ~30 titles priced in $20. By&nbsp; June that would grow to about 120 titles. ## Conclusion Between the studio holdouts, the high&nbsp; initial price of DVD player hardware,&nbsp;&nbsp; and other drama llamas like the "DivX affair", it&nbsp; took some time for the DVD boom to get started. Sales were laggy throughout 1997 and 1998.&nbsp;&nbsp; The movie studios were still in wait-and-see mode&nbsp; - though they were slowly expanding their reach.&nbsp;&nbsp; Some like Paramount would not release&nbsp; DVDs of their movies until late 1998. On the computer side, DVD-ROM drives failed&nbsp; to make much headway in part because of high&nbsp;&nbsp;

costs and Sony and Philips releasing the&nbsp; rewritable and popular CD-RW standard. By the end of December 1997, there were&nbsp; just 340,000 DVD players sold in the US&nbsp;&nbsp; compared to the expected 1.2&nbsp; million. So initial disappointment. But as the bugs got worked out, and more&nbsp; titles were released, things picked up.&nbsp;&nbsp; In April 1998, a new online service&nbsp; called Netflix launched a DVD rental&nbsp;&nbsp; by mail business, which quickly gained traction. The 1998 holiday season sold well, and then 1999&nbsp; saw volumes take off with the release of cheap&nbsp;&nbsp;

$200 DVD players from China. Momentum continued&nbsp; in 2000 with Sony's Playstation 2, a video game&nbsp;&nbsp; console that put DVD into millions of people's&nbsp; homes. Ten million were sold by March 2001. In 2001, US consumer DVD and VHS purchases&nbsp; reached parity - $5 billion each.&nbsp;&nbsp; The DVD boom - however short it would&nbsp; ultimately last - had gotten under way.
