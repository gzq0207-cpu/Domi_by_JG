## 摘要

### 全文重要观点
材料来自YouTube频道Asianometry的视频《印度 IT 外包行业的诞生与崛起》，原始链接为https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYej-KstgTg，全文时间戳覆盖0:02—31:07。原文将印度IT服务出口产业的形成归因于多项因素共同作用：Tata等企业的内部数据处理需求孕育早期服务商；设备进口必须通过软件出口赚回外汇的政策，推动企业承接软件移植业务；海外客户开拓、印度工程师与西方客户之间的工资差异，以及时区互补，为跨国交付创造商业基础；1980年代至1991年的政策改革、软件技术园区和卫星通信改善，使大量开发工作能够留在印度；UNIX技能积累与千年虫需求又放大了这一优势。千年虫项目随后转化为长期应用维护、大客户合作和更广泛的服务出口。原文后半段同时讨论离岸外包对美国就业影响的证据争议，并以2023—2024年的行业增长放缓、企业减员、股价下跌和2026年初Claude插件带来的冲击，提出生成式AI可能替代既有服务工作的风险。对于企业所称的整合、治理、信任和生产率收益能否支撑新商业模式，原文保留开放结论。以下数字与因果判断均按原文整理；所给文字稿注明未经人工校对，未提供视频发布日期及部分指标的完整统计口径。

### 论点1：TCS的前身由集团内部数据处理需求催生，但内部订单不足使其较早转向外部咨询市场。
**相应论据：**[1:00—1:43] 1960年代，Tata Group旗下钢铁、汽车等企业已有大量数据处理需求；Leslie Sawhney提出建立集团内部处理机构，获得JRD Tata批准，并从Tata Administrative Service及MIT等高校招募人员。[1:43—2:25] 1966年，该机构在孟买Nirmal Building安装两台IBM 1401和一台ICL 1903；Sawhney去世后，除Tata Electric外，集团企业交付的工作较少，主要涉及股东服务，机构因成本上升而承接外部客户。[2:25—2:55] 其咨询业务包括帮助Tata Institute of Social Sciences设计、管理和分析调查，也从IBM手中赢得客户；1968年，Tata Sons重组咨询与服务机构，成立TCS。
**相应论证：**原文将集团内部需求解释为创业起点，将关键支持者去世、内部客户接受度不足及成本压力解释为外部拓客的推动因素。外部咨询订单随后证明该机构具备独立获客能力，为TCS从内部服务单位发展为商业服务商奠定基础。原文未提供这一阶段的收入、成本或订单结构数据。

### 论点2：Kohli通过先销售管理咨询、再追加数据处理服务，解决了客户对新技术需求不足的问题。
**相应论据：**[2:55—3:28] TCS引入来自Tata Electric的PM Agarwala和F.C. Kohli；原文称早期公司潜力较大，但经营纪律尤其是现金管理不足，Kohli此前具有管理城市电网的经验。[3:28—3:43] Kohli观察公司六个月后，发现客户最初对数据处理不感兴趣，于是先推销管理咨询服务，再向客户追加销售数据处理。
**相应论证：**原文给出的经营逻辑是，先以客户能够理解和接受的管理问题建立业务关系，再引入数据处理服务，从而降低直接销售新技术的障碍。原文未展开论证这一策略对收入增长或客户转化率的具体贡献。

### 论点3：严格的外汇与进口政策既限制外资进入，也通过出口补偿要求推动印度企业形成软件出口业务。
**相应论据：**[3:43—4:18] 1973年的《外汇管理法》对进出口实施严格监管，并将合资企业外资持股比例限制为40%；Burroughs因此需要印度合作伙伴，最终与Tata Sons合作。[4:18—4:39] 1974年，TCS进口一台价值30万美元的Burroughs B1728；政府的软件出口计划要求，进口企业在随后五年内通过软件出口赚回相当于设备进口价格两倍的收入。[4:39—5:15] TCS计划通过为Burroughs开发及移植软件完成要求；软件移植约占印度外包商早期业务的20%—40%，其早期海外项目之一是价值2.4万美元的医院会计软件移植。
**相应论证：**政策将进口计算设备与获取软件出口收入绑定，使设备购买者同时成为软件出口商。Burroughs的合作关系又提供了软件迁移任务和海外客户入口。原文强调，软件移植虽然枯燥，却要求逐行细致检查代码，因而成为早期服务出口的重要业务。20%—40%是原文对早期外包业务的概括，未注明样本或具体年份。

### 论点4：TCS被迫脱离Burroughs订单依赖后，通过美国销售网络和客户背书建立了独立出口市场。
**相应论据：**[5:32—6:18] Tata于1977年获得制造Burroughs计算机的许可，但最终设立Tata Burroughs，选择进口和销售设备；其出现引发集团内部是否需要两家软件咨询机构的争议。[6:18—6:50] Kohli争取到TCS独立地位，条件是开拓Burroughs之外的业务；1970年代末，TCS在纽约开设首个办公室，由Subramanian Ramadorai负责销售。[6:50—7:24] Ramadorai获得美国银行共同持有的金融数据处理机构IGIC的大合同；印度工程师在纽约夜间工作，使开发可以全天持续，IGIC又为American Express等后续客户提供推荐。到1980年，Tata Consultancy与Tata Burroughs合计占印度约400万美元软件出口的60%以上，其余约19家公司分享剩余部分。
**相应论证：**原文将失去集团内既有订单依赖的压力，解释为独立拓客的契机。美国本地销售提供客户接触渠道，时区差异提供交付优势，已有客户的推荐则帮助缓解新客户对印度服务商的信任问题。1980年的出口数据体现早期市场高度集中，但原文未提供两家公司的单独份额。

### 论点5：IBM退出印度形成了市场进入预期，但原文明确提醒，后来出现的机会并不都直接来自IBM原有业务。
**相应论据：**[7:24—8:21] IBM自1951年进入印度后，凭硬件、软件和维护一体化租赁模式占据优势；印度政府认为其向本国提供过时设备，并要求其按FERA将印度子公司持股从100%降至40%。ICL接受要求，IBM则因全球政策坚持不降。[8:30—8:58] IBM于1977年宣布退出；已有客户支付三个月租金即可保留机器，备件和库存售予新成立的维护企业CMC，员工获得六个月工资及额外补偿。[8:58—9:13] 原文称退出突然造成空缺，但事后看，许多实际机会与IBM原先从事的业务关系不大，机会存在的感知本身已足以吸引企业进入。
**相应论证：**原文同时保留了两个层次：IBM退出改变市场格局并激发创业活动；具体企业随后找到的业务，未必是对IBM业务的直接承接。因此，不能仅凭退出事件把后续IT服务增长全部解释为填补IBM留下的市场。

### 论点6：PCS的形成体现了从低成本数据转换到设备进口、再到软件开发出口的业务演进。
**相应论据：**[9:13—9:58] Narendra Patni在美国学习和工作期间，发现学术出版中把手稿编码为数字数据的工作高度依赖人工，认为可在印度以更低成本完成，随后与妻子创办数据转换外包业务。[9:58—10:39] 1970年代的小型机趋势及IBM在印度遇到的问题，吸引DEC、Wang Labs等公司；Patni通过波士顿关系与Data General合作，于1976年和两位兄弟成立PCS，进口小型机，并按政策通过开发或移植应用软件赚回外汇。[10:39—11:05] 1979年，PCS获得50万美元合同，为Data Basics的CAMP软件进行移植和新功能开发；CAMP帮助美国企业管理来自亚洲的纺织品进口，该项目成功后带来追加业务。
**相应论证：**原文以PCS说明，跨国人工成本差异提供最初商机，海外技术企业关系提供设备和客户渠道，进口补偿要求则推动软件出口。CAMP项目的成功进一步把一次性交付转化为持续合作。原文未给出数据转换业务的实际成本节省比例。

### 论点7：Infosys从PCS的软件出口经验中孵化，创立初期因缺乏开发设备资金而采用客户现场派驻。
**相应论据：**[11:05—11:35] N. R. Narayana Murthy于1977年加入PCS，担任软件业务总经理，同时承担数据中心管理、印度计算机销售及软件出口销售；他此前在印度创办软件公司失败。[11:35—12:04] 四年工作和美国市场接触后，Murthy与六位同事创业，使用妻子提供的250美元，于1981年7月成立Infosys，并争取到PCS的重要客户Data Basics。[12:04—12:16] 由于买不起自己的计算机，除Murthy外的联合创始人均赴美国客户现场工作。
**相应论证：**原文把行业经验、海外客户认知和既有客户关系列为Infosys创业基础，同时指出设备资本不足决定了初期交付形式。现场派驻在此既是服务出口方式，也是缺乏本土开发设备条件下的现实选择。

### 论点8：1980年代的进口与软件政策放松降低了出口开发门槛，监管目标也逐渐从保护硬件制造转向促进软件出口。
**相应论据：**[12:16—13:04] 原文认为，自给自足导向的政策到1980年代中期已造成增长受限和经济隔离；Rajiv Gandhi于1984年出任总理后推动技术相关改革。[13:04—13:46] 用于软件开发的进口个人电脑关税从135%降至60%，数年后降至零；用于开发出口软件的软件工具可免税进口。部分改革后来因经济与政治不稳定而回撤，包括提高软件和海外旅行税负，但软件出口仍从约十年前的350万美元增至1990年的9600万美元。[13:47—14:06] 电子部的角色从监管电子生产、保护ECIL等本土制造商，转向促进软件出口和私人投资。
**相应论证：**原文将开发工具与设备进口成本下降，以及监管目标调整，视为软件出口加速的条件，同时明确改革并非单向推进。出口起点存在原文口径差异：此处称约1980年为350万美元，[7:10]则称1980年为400万美元；原文未解释差异，不能将两者强行合并。

### 论点9：软件技术园区沿用了出口加工区制度，其关键增量是为软件企业提供更好的跨国通信和共享基础设施。
**相应论据：**[14:06—14:57] 印度此前已有设备进口免税、劳动规定较宽松的出口加工区，包括Gujarat的KASEZ和1973年成立、原面向电子制造的孟买Santacruz Electronics EPZ；Citibank的软件子公司、TCS、Tata Burroughs及Gateway Design Automation等进入其中。[14:57—15:11] 官员提出专门促进软件出口的园区，除延续免税和劳动政策外，最重要的是改善与美国的通信连接。[16:42—16:58] 电子部认为共享计算、数据通信及其他基础设施有助于软件出口，于1986年开始推动软件技术园区。
**相应论证：**原文强调，软件园区的作用不仅是税收优惠，还包括集中提供企业难以独立获得的计算与国际通信条件。其针对的是软件开发跨国协作的实际约束。原文未量化各项优惠与基础设施对出口增长的分别贡献。

### 论点10：落后的通信系统使印度软件出口长期依赖人员赴海外现场工作。
**相应论据：**[15:11—15:34] 原文称当时印度电话系统落后、容量不足且效率低，固定电话接入等待名单达50万人，通常需等待2—5年。[15:34—15:48] 在缺乏可靠通信的情况下，印度IT出口只能大量依靠现场派驻；直到1980年代末，这类业务仍贡献75%—80%的出口收入。
**相应论证：**原文给出的约束是，远程传递代码和协调开发困难，使工作更容易随工程师移动到客户所在地。通信问题因此限制了低成本印度本土开发的扩大。75%—80%的分母是原文所称出口收入，并非员工比例。

### 论点11：Texas Instruments的卫星连接项目示范了跨国远程开发的可行性，也暴露了通信监管改革的阻力。
**相应论据：**[15:48—16:26] Texas Instruments于1985年建立美国与Bangalore之间的卫星数据链路，意在利用印度人才、市场潜力及时区差异实现全天开发；电子部积极协调，一位部门人士称为该项目突破了26项规则。[16:26—16:42] 按法律，只有电信部可以拥有和运营地面站设备，因此安排由Texas Instruments出资、部门运营并出租设备。[16:51—17:12] 软件技术园区虽于1986年开始推动，但由于关键通信连接的部门争执，直到1991年才实际落地。
**相应论证：**原文通过这一案例说明，远程开发既需要通信技术，也需要解决设备所有权、运营权和部门协调问题。个别项目的制度安排先行，随后才发展为可供更多企业使用的园区基础设施。关于突破26项规则的表述是原文引用的官员说法，未列出完整规则清单。

### 论点12：可靠通信使离岸交付模式扩大，印度工资优势得以在本土开发中发挥，同时降低了现场派驻限制带来的压力。
**相应论据：**[17:12—17:45] 原文称，现场派驻声誉不佳，1990年代初美国B-1商务访问签证规则变化及优秀员工流失风险，使其更难持续；TCS所称离岸模式由小团队赴客户现场收集需求、实施方案，大部分开发留在印度，当地工资约为西方的三分之一。[17:45—18:04] TCS早期已采用类似安排，但代码需由乘飞机的人携带文件袋传递，园区卫星通信释放了该模式潜力。[18:04—18:19] 通信改善还支持业务流程外包和呼叫中心等服务；现场工作占比由1980年代末的80%以上，降至1990年代末的59%。
**相应论证：**原文将更快的信息交换与本土开发占比提升直接联系：客户沟通和实施保留少量现场人员，主要劳动投入放在较低工资地区。此处现场工作占比的起点为80%以上，与[15:34]的75%—80%表述不同，且此处未再次明确分母；原文未解释差异，也未量化通信改善、签证变化与员工流失因素的各自影响。

### 论点13：1991年自由化同时改善了外资进入、企业融资和本土服务商扩张的条件。
**相应论据：**[18:19—18:46] 国际收支危机触发1991年改革，内容包括拆除原有许可管制体系、卢比贬值及降低外资壁垒；Oracle、IBM、HP等外国企业由此可以在印度设立自己的软件开发机构。[18:46—18:58] 改革也使Infosys等公司能够筹集资本，Infosys于1993年上市，并用募集资金建设园区。
**相应论证：**原文将自由化解释为供给条件的系统变化：外国企业可以直接组织印度开发活动，本土企业则获得扩建设施的资本渠道。原文未分别计算汇率、外资制度与融资改革对行业增长的贡献。

### 论点14：进口硬件竞争促使Wipro和HCL从本土硬件业务转向服务出口，塑造了后来的大型服务商格局。
**相应论据：**[18:58—19:31] 贸易壁垒下降带来的海外硬件涌入，迫使Wipro等调整业务；Wipro原为植物油生产商，在IBM退出后进入小型机和个人电脑领域，1991年后转向为电信设备和计算机开发嵌入式应用，再进入银行、零售和制造业的通用软件开发。[19:31—20:03] HCL最初生产计算器和个人电脑，在进口冲击后转向软件移植、驱动开发和现场服务，后来剥离硬件业务。原文将Wipro、HCL/HCLTech、Tech Mahindra与TCS、Infosys并列为主要IT服务出口巨头，并称这些巨头90%以上收入来自海外。
**相应论证：**原文的因果链是，硬件市场开放削弱原有本土制造业务的竞争空间，相关工程与编程能力随后转向海外服务市场。最终形成的服务商高度依赖海外收入。90%以上是原文对这些企业的概括，未给出逐家公司、逐年份的口径。

[0:02] It is perhaps one of history's most unlikely industrial success stories.
[0:06] India was desperately poor - a largely agricultural nation with weak infrastructure running a heavily regulated socialist economic model.
[0:15] Yet somehow, a number of billion dollar Indian tech giants managed to emerge. And through them, India became a monster IT service export powerhouse.
[0:27] Offshoring continues to strike strong emotions today. In today’s video, we look at the rise of the Indian IT export industry.
[0:35] ## Coauthors Before we start, I want to thank my coauthor, Zoso/Felix of the Zoso Project.
[0:42] I have been following his X feed for some time now and am impressed by his unique perspective. I don't always agree but I have to think about it first.
[0:52] Go check out his work, which includes the companion pieces to this video - headed by this provocative image. Alright on with the show.
[1:00] ## Beginnings By the 1960s, India's Tata Group had grown into a massive industrial giant with firms like the Tata Iron and Steel Company and Tata Motors.
[1:10] These companies had significant data processing needs. Seeing how the West increasingly valued
[1:16] such data processing work, Chairman JRD Tata's brother-in-law, aide, and friend Colonel Leslie Sawhney proposed an in-house unit to process data for all the Tata Group’s companies.
[1:29] JRD approved and the new, then unnamed firm was seeded with staff from Tata Administrative Service. They hired several talented, quantitatively minded folks from MIT and other top universities.
[1:43] In 1966, they moved into one of India's first skyscrapers - the Nirmal Building in south Bombay - and set up two IBM 1401s and an ICL 1903. Thus began the Tata Computer Center.
[1:58] But soon afterwards, Sawhney passed - leaving the new firm rudder-less and without its key champion within the larger Tata conglomerate.
[2:06] The Tata firms listened to presentations but remained weary of the new unit and passed relatively little work, mostly relating to shareholder services.
[2:16] The only exception being Tata Electric - the group's power utility. With costs rising, the unit began taking on external clients.
[2:25] Their consulting work spanned the gamut. For instance, they advised the Tata Institute of
[2:30] Social Sciences on how to structure, administer and analyze surveys - but whatever they were doing they were good enough to win customers away from IBM.
[2:41] In 1968, Tata Sons - the group's core holding company - restructured their consulting and
[2:47] service firms into four operating units. One of these units was Tata Consultancy Services or TCS.
[2:55] Afterwards, TCS received two top managers from Tata Electric - Director PM Agarwala and General Manager Faqir Chand (F.C.) Kohli.
[3:05] ## Tata Consultancy Services TCS began as a young rowdy company with lots of potential but perhaps not the most discipline, especially with cash.
[3:13] In light of that, Kohli - a brilliant technocrat who cut his teeth running the city's power grid - was the right person to bring on. He initially did not want to come
[3:22] run TCS - he eyed the head job at Tata Electric - but was eventually coerced.
[3:28] Kohli studied the firm for six months, then made a fateful change. As customer companies were not initially interested in data processing,
[3:36] TCS first pitched them management consulting services and upsold the data processing later.
[3:43] In 1973, Indira Gandhi's government - then suffering a fiscal deficit - passed the
[3:48] Foreign Exchange Regulation Act. FERA imposed strict regulations on imports and exports. Moreover, it famously restricted foreign ownership in JVs to only 40%.
[4:01] One of the companies affected was the American firm Burroughs. The firm's managers wanted to
[4:06] enter India, but needed a partner. After building a strong personal relationship with Kohli, they chose to partner with Tata Sons for a JV producing Burroughs software and hardware.
[4:18] So in 1974, TCS imported one of Burroughs' computers, a B1728 costing $300,000.
[4:27] The government's Software Export Scheme allowed companies to import such hardware, but it also committed them to earning back double the import price in software export revenues over the next five years.
[4:39] TCS intended to earn that back developing software for Burroughs - including migrating older software to new hardware platforms.
[4:48] Porting was a major initial category for the Indian IT outsourcers. Drab and tedious,
[4:54] yet also requiring paying close attention to every line in the code. It contributed some 20-40% of their early business.
[5:03] One of their first overseas engagements was a $24,000 project porting a hospital accounting package written in Burroughs medium systems COBOL to Burroughs small systems COBOL.
[5:15] This project and others were delivered using a combination of development work done in India
[5:21] and onsite efforts done by TCS workers flown right to the United States. This latter practice would become widespread in the Indian IT export industry and is today known as "body shopping".
[5:32] ## Setting Up In the United States As I briefly mentioned earlier, Tata struck a joint venture with Burroughs to import and make their hardware.
[5:39] In 1977, the Indian government finally granted Tata the license to manufacture Burroughs computers. But after all that work, the Tata Sons office decided against manufacturing computers.
[5:51] Instead, they set up a separate JV company called Tata Burroughs that would import and sell Burroughs computers into India. To some, this was a missed opportunity for an Indian company to actually start making computer hardware. They kind of took the easy way out.
[6:07] Anyway. Tata Burroughs' sudden appearance threatened TCS' existence. Why have two separate software and consulting houses operating inside Tata?
[6:18] But FC Kohli pushed back hard, and it was eventually agreed that TCS can retain its independence. However it had to find its own book of business outside of the Burroughs work. With this, a challenge became an opportunity.
[6:32] In the late 1970s, TCS set up its first New York office and sent over Subramanian
[6:38] Ramadorai - a rising star in the organization - to be the sales manager. Armed with little but a thin portfolio of Burroughs work, Ramadorai diligently typed letters and sought business.
[6:50] The first big contract he landed was with the Institutional Group Information Corporation, an American financial data processing consortium owned by banks.
[7:00] Indian engineers can work while New York slept - enabling a 24-hour dev cycle. IGIC became a willing reference for future customers like American Express.
[7:10] By 1980, Tata Consultancy and Tata Burroughs had emerged as India's two top software exporters - accounting for over 60% of the $4 million of exports. 19 or so firms shared the rest.
[7:24] ## IBM's Departure Since 1951 when it founded a marketing and support office in Calcutta, IBM had been India’s dominant computer company.
[7:33] In the 1960s, the company rented digital computers like its IBM 1401 using its usual full-service rental model encompassing hardware, software, and maintenance. They lapped the field in terms of computer counts.
[7:47] But the government grew dissatisfied - feeling that IBM treated their country as a "dumping ground" for old, outdated machines. The company negotiated with the Department of
[7:57] Electronics on items like tech transfer, but eventually came to a non-negotiable matter.
[8:03] That matter was IBM owning 100% share of its Indian subsidiary.
[8:08] Per the aforementioned FERA law, that share must be diluted down to 40%. Britain's ICL, the other large foreign computer company, complied but IBM stood their ground.
[8:21] It had less to do with control - IBM can still exercise full control with only 40% - than the fact that it was their global policy.
[8:30] In 1977, things came to a head, and IBM finally announced that it will leave. Other companies like the boomer drink Coca-Cola did the same.
[8:40] Customers who already had IBM machines were allowed to keep them after paying 3 months' rent.
[8:46] Spares and inventory were sold off to a newly formed maintenance company called CMC. And IBM India employees got six months of salary plus more as a goodbye.
[8:58] IBM's departure abruptly created a void that many tried to fill. In hindsight,
[9:04] many of the actual opportunities that emerged had little to do with what IBM once did. But the perception that such opportunities existed was enough.
[9:13] ## Patni and Patni Computer Systems Narendra Patni was a clean-cut man with a big smile and relaxed look.
[9:20] The son of a grain merchant, he did his undergraduate studies at India's elite University of Roorkee. Then in 1964, he received a scholarship to go to the United States for a masters at MIT.
[9:33] Whilst working at Forrester Consulting Group, a marketing and research firm, Patni got interested in academic publishing. He noticed how manual the process of encoding manuscripts to digital data, and thought that it could be done in India for less.
[9:49] After a trial run with India and America represented by separate bedrooms, Patni and his wife founded an outsourcing business for data conversion.
[9:58] Then in the 1970s, IBM’s troubles in India plus changing business conditions spurred a shift to smaller minicomputers. DEC, Wang Labs and others itched to enter the Indian market.
[10:12] Through his Boston connections, Patni struck a partnership with Data General - an early
[10:17] minicomputer firm. So in 1976, he and his two brothers founded a new company called Patni Computer Systems or PCS to import these minicomputers.
[10:29] Per Indian policy, PCS had to "earn back" this hardware with software exports, so they wrote or ported application software for clients.
[10:39] In 1979, PCS won a $500,000 deal porting and developing new features for a software application called comprehensive apparel manufacturer’s package (CAMP).
[10:51] Made by a firm called Data Basics, CAMP was a kind of proto-ERP that US companies used to manage textile imports from Asia. The engagement was successful and led to additional work with Data Basics.
[11:05] ## Patni to Infosys The man leading the effort at PCS was N. R. Narayana Murthy, hired back in 1977 to be general manager of PCS' growing software business.
[11:18] A talented programmer and devout Hindu, Murthy had once before started his own company
[11:24] selling software in India but failed. At PCS, he handled three jobs at once: Data center management, computer sales in India, and software export sales.
[11:35] After four years of doing this and visiting and understanding the US market, he felt that he was ready to try starting another company. And believed that if he didn't do it before turning 35, he would be cooked.
[11:48] Six of his colleagues ended up joining him. Funded with $250 from Murthy's wife, they started Infosys in July 1981. The new business
[11:58] quickly won over PCS's best customer Data Basics, which sold the CAMP software.
[12:04] But in those early days, Infosys did not have enough money to buy their own computer to do software development. So the company sent all of its cofounders except Murthy to America to work physically at the client site. More body-shopping.
[12:16] ## Changing Times: A Focus on Software India's IT sector grew within a socialist-minded, nationalist framework that emphasized self-reliance.
[12:25] Nothing wrong with that, but there comes a line when self-reliance tips into autarky. By the mid-1980s, the Indian economy was suffering from stifled growth and isolation.
[12:36] Then came a series of major developments that unlocked and supercharged the IT exports industry.
[12:43] First, we had government reform. In 1984, Rajiv Gandhi was elected as Prime Minister after his mother's assassination.
[12:51] Gandhi was technically savvy - a booster of technology - and experienced first-hand the issues of India's electronics policies whilst organizing the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi.
[13:04] His administration passed a series of new liberal policies favoring software and hardware imports.
[13:11] Tariffs on PCs imported for developing software were cut from 135% to just 60%, and then zero a
[13:19] few years later. Software tools for developing other export software can be imported duty-free.
[13:26] Many of these liberalizations were somewhat walked back due to unstable economic and political
[13:32] situations. Taxes on software and foreign travel were hiked back up, for instance. But what stuck helped software exports reach $96 million in 1990 - up from just $3.5 million ten years earlier.
[13:47] Once upon a time, India's Department of Electronics saw its role as regulating electronics production and protecting domestic manufacturing - i.e.
[13:56] coddling India's biggest computer manufacturer, ECIL. But in the 1980s, that attitude shifted to promoting software exports and private investment.
[14:06] ## Changing Times: Software Parks Second were the Software Technology Parks, or STPs.
[14:11] The concept of an export processing zone enjoying duty-free privileges on imported equipment and relaxed labor statutes isn't new even within India.
[14:21] India was in fact one of the first Asian countries to have them with the KASEZ in Gujarat.
[14:27] A particularly special one was the Santacruz Electronics EPZ in Mumbai. Created back in 1973 for electronics manufacturing, a few software companies made their way there.
[14:39] Citibank opened a software development subsidiary inside. TCS and Tata Burroughs too.
[14:46] The EDA firm Gateway Design Automation was there too in be 1980s - they are perhaps most known for producing the hardware design language Verilog.
[14:57] Some government officials started thinking about a special zone to encourage software exports. They would have the same duty-free and labor privileges, but most importantly the zones would have better communication links with the US.
[15:11] Few things more personified India's issues than its massive communications problem. The Indian telecommunications sector was just not good. At the time it was perhaps
[15:21] the world's most archaic, inadequate, overloaded and inefficient phone system. The waiting list for a landline telephone connection stretched half a million long, and took 2-5 years of waiting.
[15:34] Without more robust communications, the Indian IT exports industry had no choice but to rely on body-shopping. Up into the late 1980s, 75-80% of India's export earnings still came from body-shopping.
[15:48] So it was a big deal when Texas Instruments in 1985 installed a satellite data link between the US and the Indian city of Bangalore.
[15:58] They saw India as a good potential market, a place for talent, and they wanted to use the time zone differences to achieve a 24-hour development cycle.
[16:07] To get this satellite link installed, the Indian Department of Electronics went to
[16:12] bat for Texas Instruments. Hard. One DOE veteran proudly said that they "broke 26 separate rules to accommodate TI's Bangalore subsidiary and are willing to break more".
