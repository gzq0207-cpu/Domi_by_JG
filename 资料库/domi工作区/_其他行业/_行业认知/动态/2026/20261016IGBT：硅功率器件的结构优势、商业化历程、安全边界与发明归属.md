## 摘要

### 全文重要观点
这份 Asianometry 文字稿围绕绝缘栅双极型晶体管（IGBT）展开，解释其如何把功率场效应晶体管的电压控制方式与双极结晶体管的双极导电机制结合起来，在高电压、大电流应用中取得实用价值。全文的重要线索包括：晶闸管、功率双极结晶体管和功率场效应晶体管各自的局限；IGBT通过反型沟道和少数载流子注入降低导通电阻的工作机制；少数载流子清除造成的关断延迟，以及寄生晶体管造成的锁存风险；General Electric、RCA与Toshiba分别在概念提出、商业化和无锁存设计上的贡献；发明归属为何取决于对IGBT的定义；以及其在变频家电、风力发电和电动汽车中的应用。结尾认为，尽管碳化硅和氮化镓功率器件开始崛起，硅IGBT仍主导中等功率档位。来源：https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pGQWq_q1gnM，视频时长1382秒。以下依据用户提供的全部文字稿整理；字幕未人工校对，原文关于早期上市年份存在口径差异。

### 论点1：功率半导体是电力电子的基础开关，其核心性能要求是在低导通损耗、高效阻断和快速切换之间取得兼顾。
**相应论据：**[0:40–1:20] 原文指出，电力电子负责控制电能流动，其中的功率半导体与中央处理器中的晶体管一样承担开关功能，但控制的电流和功率大得多。与机械开关相比，半导体没有会磨损的运动部件，寿命更长、切换安静，每秒可以切换数千次乃至数百万次。[1:34–1:40] 理想开关在导通时应像金属一样高导电、损耗极低，在关断时应像绝缘体一样阻断电流，并能迅速在两种状态之间切换。
**相应论证：**原文先从电力电子的功能界定功率半导体的作用，再以机械开关作为比较对象说明其优势，最后提出评价器件的三个基本维度。这些要求构成后文比较晶闸管、双极结晶体管、功率场效应晶体管和IGBT的共同尺度。每秒数千至数百万次是对功率半导体的概括，不能直接视为全部IGBT的开关频率。

### 论点2：普通晶闸管的主要局限是导通后会锁存，尤其在直流场景中难以直接按需关断。
**相应论据：**[1:52–2:23] 晶闸管由交替的P型和N型材料构成PNPN四层结构，具有阳极、阴极和栅极；General Electric最初以硅控整流器的名称推出这种器件。[2:34–2:58] 晶闸管一旦导通，会持续导电，直到阳极电流降为零或低于某个限值。交流整流时，电流会随交变过程经过零点；直流电流则不会自然经过零点，因此需要额外电路强制降低阳极电流，增加功耗和体积。[3:08–3:16] 这一问题推动了可按需开关的器件研究，门极可关断晶闸管是其中一例，但以开关速度为代价。
**相应论证：**原文的因果链是：锁存使栅极无法独立完成关断；交流的过零条件可以帮助关断，直流则缺少这一条件；额外关断电路带来系统代价。因此，能够通过控制端直接决定开关状态成为后续功率器件的重要研发目标。

### 论点3：功率双极结晶体管能够按需开关，但持续基极驱动、少数载流子清除和过热风险限制了其实用性。
**相应论据：**[3:23–3:53] 双极结晶体管由发射极、基极和集电极构成，输入基极电流控制集电极与发射极之间的电流；保持导通需要持续提供基极电流，因此消耗能量。[4:06–5:09] 原文解释，双极器件依赖多数和少数载流子，进入饱和后，关断需要清除注入的少数载流子，因而速度较慢，在电动机等应用中较难使用。[5:21–5:29] 此类器件还容易过热，工程师可能需要增加保护电路，从而增加体积。
**相应论证：**原文从驱动、动态响应和承受应力的能力三个方面解释局限：持续电流驱动产生能耗；储存的少数载流子使关断不能立即完成；过热敏感性又增加保护需求。由此说明，能够自主关断并不代表已经同时满足低驱动损耗、快速开关和可靠运行的要求。

### 论点4：垂直结构让功率场效应晶体管突破早期低功率限制，但原文认为其高电压能力仍不及功率双极结晶体管。
**相应论据：**[5:34–6:04] 原文称，早期场效应晶体管只能处理不足1瓦的很低功率，主要用于信号切换；20世纪70年代末出现的垂直扩散等新结构改变了这一情况。[6:04–6:24] 横向器件的栅极、源极和漏极位于同一平面，垂直功率器件则把栅极和源极放在硅体顶部、漏极放在底部。功率场效应晶体管可用电压控制，开关更快，也更耐受应力。[6:36–7:04] 原文提到1977年Siliconix Limited的J.O.M. Jenkins发表文章讨论其对功率双极器件的挑战，但随后指出，它处理高电压的能力不如功率双极结晶体管，适用于个人计算机等较小功率设备，而不适合当时所讨论的电动汽车或机车等更高功率用途。
**相应论证：**原文用结构变化解释功率能力提升，再将其电压控制、速度和耐受性优势，与高电压能力的不足并列，说明两类器件各有所长。这一判断处于全文叙述的早期技术演进背景，原文没有给出具体耐压、电流或器件代际参数。

### 论点5：IGBT的研发出发点，是把场效应器件的控制优势与双极器件的高功率能力结合起来。
**相应论据：**[7:09–7:47] B. Jayant Baliga于1974年加入General Electric的企业研发中心；1975年Jun-ichi Nishizawa公布静电感应晶闸管后，General Electric安排Baliga开发类似器件。[7:47–8:04] 他经过约三年做出相关器件，但这些器件默认导通，原文认为这使其不适合功率应用。[8:04–8:15] 面对功率双极结晶体管与功率场效应晶体管各自的缺陷，Baliga提出，能否开发一种采用电压控制、具有场效应器件开关速度和耐受性、同时能够像双极器件一样处理高功率的半导体。[8:28–8:38] 原文将IGBT描述为两类器件特征的结合。
**相应论证：**原文把IGBT的出现归结为已有技术路线的性能缺口，以及默认导通器件的研发受阻。结合两种器件是研发目标；后文也明确指出，实际IGBT仍存在少数载流子导致的关断延迟，因此不能把最初目标理解为已经无条件获得了全部理想性能。

### 论点6：IGBT的高电压阻断能力与其垂直结构中厚而轻掺杂的漂移区有关。
**相应论据：**[8:51–9:16] 原文把IGBT描述为具有垂直双极器件主体、由场效应结构控制的器件。它具有集电极、发射极和栅极三个端子，集电极位于底部；在关断状态下，电子不能从发射极流向集电极，厚而轻掺杂的非导电漂移区有助于阻断高电压。
**相应论证：**原文将器件结构与关断功能直接对应：关断时需要阻断电流并承受电压，漂移区承担其中的高电压阻断作用。原文未进一步给出漂移区厚度、掺杂浓度与耐压之间的定量关系。

### 论点7：IGBT通过栅极形成导电沟道，再触发双极注入和电导调制，从而实现大电流导通。
**相应论据：**[9:27–9:59] 以原文所称更主流的N沟道IGBT为例，栅极电压吸引电子进入其下方的P型体区；达到阈值电压后，体区表面形成反型层，允许电子从发射极流入N型漂移区。[10:05–10:28] 这股电子流类似于给PNP双极结晶体管提供基极电流，促使集电极上方P型层向N型漂移区注入空穴，空穴在该区域属于少数载流子。[10:39] 电子与空穴共同产生电导调制，使电阻显著下降，允许大电流通过。
**相应论证：**原文给出的机制依次是：栅极电压形成反型沟道，沟道提供电子流，电子流触发空穴注入，增加的两类载流子降低漂移区电阻。它由此解释IGBT如何把电压控制方式与双极导电机制集成在一个器件中。

### 论点8：IGBT虽然采用电压控制，但仍继承了双极器件因少数载流子储存而产生的关断延迟。
**相应论据：**[10:53–11:02] 将栅极电压降到阈值以下，可以关闭发射极到集电极的电子流；但漂移区中的少数载流子仍需要时间清除，因此IGBT也存在与双极结晶体管相同类型的开关时间问题。
**相应论证：**关闭栅极所控制的电子通道，与清除此前注入漂移区的少数载流子，是原文区分的两个过程。大电流导通依赖的载流子注入，也形成关断速度的约束。原文没有给出统一的关断时间，也没有声称所有型号的速度相同。

### 论点9：General Electric内部的变速驱动需求和跨业务应用前景，推动了Baliga方案从研究走向产品。
**相应论据：**[11:12] Baliga于1977年首次记录其混合器件构想，并于1979年9月在Electronics Letters发表。[11:31–12:08] General Electric一位副总裁希望替换热泵和空调可调速驱动中的功率双极结晶体管；Baliga提出，该方案还可用于家电、照明等其他业务。[12:08–12:28] Jack Welch于1980年末推动项目加速，首批晶圆于1981年8月产出；Baliga于1982年12月在IEDM公开展示，称为Insulated Gate Rectifier，产品随后于1983年6月以Insulated Gate Transistor或IGT名称上市。原文还称其获得Electronic Products年度产品奖。
**相应论证：**原文把具体产品部门的替代需求、研发团队提出的多场景用途，以及管理层推动联系起来，解释研究概念如何获得商业化资源。需要保留时间口径差异：[19:16]又称Baliga于1982年率先将IGR推向市场，与此处1982年公开展示、1983年IGT上市的叙述不同；原文没有进一步解释这些名称与年份的对应关系。

### 论点10：RCA独立推进了相似的混合功率器件，说明早期商业化由多个团队共同推动。
**相应论据：**[12:39–12:57] 原文称，RCA在20世纪70年代的新产品需要更高电压和更大功率，Carl F. Wheatley因此寻找新一代功率器件。[12:57–13:13] Wheatley在2010年接受Electronic Design采访时回忆，希望让功率场效应晶体管同时具有双极作用，以获得高电流密度能力。[13:13–13:28] Hans Becke负责进一步完善并形成专利，两人于1980年3月提交专利申请，数年后获批；RCA于1983年将器件商业化，命名为COMFET，并发表论文。[19:02] 原文明示General Electric与RCA团队独立工作。
**相应论证：**原文以不同企业的产品需求、独立研发和专利记录，说明这一技术并非只有单一推进路径。COMFET、IGR、IGT和IGBT等多种名称，也增加了追踪早期技术与产品历史的难度。

### 论点11：早期IGBT的关键可靠性问题是寄生晶体管引发的锁存，严重时会失去栅极控制并发生热失控。
**相应论据：**[13:43–14:06] 原文指出，General Electric与RCA的早期器件都存在锁存问题，IGBT与晶闸管一样包含PNPN结构。IGBT正常工作可理解为场效应晶体管驱动PNP双极结晶体管，但内部还存在不希望出现的寄生NPN双极结晶体管。[14:18–14:43] 从集电极注入的空穴经过漂移区和体区流向发射极金属接触，横向流经体区时产生电阻压降；电流及压降足够大时，会触发寄生NPN器件。[14:43–14:50] 寄生NPN与正常PNP形成自维持回路，关闭栅极也无法使器件关断，并可能导致热失控。
**相应论证：**原文完整说明了从空穴电流、体区电阻压降到寄生器件导通，再到正反馈自维持的过程。锁存的严重性在于器件不再服从正常栅极关断，而不只是损耗增加或速度变慢。

### 论点12：Baliga的Deep P+结构降低了早期器件的锁存倾向，但没有使其在全部工作范围内消除锁存风险。
**相应论据：**[15:01–15:11] 面对包括同事在内的质疑，Baliga在晶体管内部引入名为Deep P+的掺杂硅区域；原文称该区域限制空穴流动，使锁存更难发生。[15:18] 原文随即强调，General Electric的IGR、IGT与RCA的COMFET仍可能出现锁存，用户需要确保器件处于规定的工作窗口之内。
**相应论证：**原文明确区分了提高锁存发生难度与消除锁存这两个层次。Deep P+是早期风险缓解措施，系统使用者仍须遵守安全工作边界。原文未提供其可提高多少锁存电流或适用哪些具体型号。

### 论点13：硅辐照后处理被用于改善关断速度，并使早期器件可以针对不同频率和用途进行调整。
**相应论据：**[15:29] Baliga引入硅辐照后处理，制造晶格缺陷；原文称这些缺陷可以在IGBT栅极关断后捕获电子，从而加快切换。[15:45–15:54] 原文将General Electric推出空调用可调速电机与这些研发成果联系起来，并称首批IGR可以针对60赫兹、10千赫兹乃至100千赫兹进行调整，适配电机驱动到不间断电源等应用。
**相应论证：**原文通过改变载流子相关行为来解释开关速度改善，并以不同频率和应用说明器件能够按用途调整。60赫兹、10千赫兹和100千赫兹是原文列出的可调整频率，不能据此认定同一器件在全部频率下具有相同的损耗、电流或可靠性表现；这些性能差异原文未展开论证。

### 论点14：较慢的开关速度并不必然否定IGBT的应用价值，关键在于具体大电流场景是否能够接受。
**相应论据：**[16:04–16:26] 原文称，1982年IGR首次展示时，许多日本公司持怀疑态度。Toshiba的Akio Nakagawa最初也质疑，为何要关注开关时间超过10微秒的器件。[16:26–16:34] 返回日本后，他认识到存在许多可以容忍较慢开关速度的大电流应用。[16:40–16:46] 1983年初，他被安排把双极器件与场效应晶体管集成到同一芯片上，随后着手开发自己的IGR或IGT并解决锁存问题。
**相应论证：**原文以Nakagawa判断的变化说明，单独比较开关速度不足以决定器件价值，还需要考虑目标应用的大电流需求及其允许的速度范围。原文未列出这些可容忍较慢速度应用的完整清单，也未量化其规模。

### 论点15：Nakagawa解决锁存问题的关键，是把器件饱和电流限制在锁存电流以下，而不是仅提高锁存门槛。
**相应论据：**[16:55–17:16] 原文区分两种电流限值：锁存电流限值是触发寄生晶闸管行为的边界，饱和电流限值是器件物理上能够承载的最大电流；此前的主要思路是提高锁存电流限值。[17:23–17:38] 原文称双极器件的饱和电流限值较高，容易达到锁存条件，而且锁存限值会随因素变化，焦耳热导致的高温是重要因素。[17:49–17:59] Nakagawa将饱和电流降到锁存电流以下，使器件先达到电流上限，锁存点则位于正常工作范围之外。
**相应论证：**原文的论证核心是改变两种限值的相对关系：如果器件能够达到的电流先被限制，正常工作中就不会继续增大到触发锁存的水平。这使安全性从依赖用户避开某一区域，转向器件自身特性约束。原文没有给出完整设计参数、温度范围或验证数据。

### 论点16：在原文叙述中，全工作范围内一致、无锁存的行为，是IGBT扩大中高压变流器应用的重要条件。
**相应论据：**[18:05–18:11] Toshiba于1985年开始销售Nakagawa改进后的IGBT。原文称，器件在整个工作范围内具有一致行为后，迅速在许多中高压变流器应用中替代功率双极结晶体管和功率场效应晶体管，同时继续与其他器件形成互补。

[10:23] 空穴从集电极正上方的一层 P 型材料中注入，
[10:28] 进入 N 型漂移区，在那里它们是少数载流子。事实上，很多空穴会一路向上到达发射极金属层。记住这一点。

[15:45] 得益于 Baliga 的工作，GE 为空调推出了一种新型可调速电机——这是一项新业务。此外，首批下线的 IGR 可以调节至从

[0:02] The insulated-gate bipolar transistor or IGBT is a juggernaut in the power silicon world.
[0:08] Offering unprecedented control over high power, it has transformed the worlds of energy generation, EVs, transport, industry, and beyond.
[0:18] The really weird thing is what it is. It’s not a Bipolar Junction Transistor. Nor is it a MOSFET. It’s both.
[0:26] Today's video has it all. Shiny silicon. High voltage power. Hot thermal runaway. Sexy vertical transistors. Electric disputes over who invented what. Today, the IGBT.
[0:40] ## Beginnings Power electronics control the flow of power and make it useful.
[0:47] Inside those power electronics are power semiconductors. Such semiconductors are switches just like the transistors inside your CPU. It is just that they
[0:56] switch way larger, more powerful currents than what those CPU transistors switch.
[1:02] Why use power semiconductor switches rather than say, an air-gapped one? Unlike mechanical
[1:07] switches, power semiconductors lack moving parts that wear down. They last far longer. They switch without making noise and at a far faster rate. Thousands or even millions of times per second.
[1:20] Power electronics are a core part of our electric infrastructure. And improving power semiconductors have played major roles in commercializing
[1:28] certain life-changing inventions. Yet nobody talks about them. Kinda shocking isn’t it?
[1:34] So a power semiconductor is a switch. What do you want from a good switch? When ON, it must be
[1:40] like a metal - very conductive with minimal loss. When OFF, it must be an insulator - interrupting current. And ideally, it should be able to toggle between the two states very fast.
[1:52] ## Them Legendary Thyristors The power semiconductor field emerged seventy years ago with the legendary silicon thyristor.
[1:59] The thyristor consists of four layers of alternating P and N-type materials, making it a PNPN device. It is equipped with three terminals - an anode, cathode, and gate.
[2:11] By default, the thyristor's gate is closed. But apply a small current to the gate terminal, then it opens and now a current can travel in one direction from the anode to the cathode.
[2:23] First introduced by General Electric as the Silicon Controlled Rectifier, the thyristor is and remains a genuine solid-state breakthrough. But it was not perfect.
[2:34] Its most significant limitation was that when the device gets turned on, it latches. In other words, it continues to conduct until the current at the anode side falls to zero or below some limit. Only then does the thyristor close again.
[2:48] If we are using the thyristor as a rectifier turning AC to DC, then that is fine. The AC current will eventually hit zero when it flips and crosses over.
[2:58] But a DC current doesn’t hit zero. Thus additional circuitry must be added to force down the anode current - which eats up power and space.
[3:08] This shortcoming motivated work into new power semiconductors that allow us to toggle the gate open or closed at will.
[3:16] One major example being the Gate Turn-Off Thyristor, which offers that on-demand switching at the cost of switching speed.
[3:23] ## Power Bipolar Junction Transistors Thus arose power Bipolar junction transistors. They are one of the first practical transistors to hit the market.
[3:31] An ordinary BJT has three sections of alternatively doped semiconductor material: The emitter, base, and collector. An input drive current flowing into the base controls another current between the collector and emitter.
[3:46] While power BJTs can switch on and off at will using the input base current, they suffered their own issues.
[3:53] First and foremost, the bipolar transistor is - for all practical purposes - a current-controlled device. So holding the transistor on requires a continuous base current. This consumes energy.
[4:06] A second concern is speed. The BJT does not switch particularly fast. And when it hits saturation, it takes some extra time to close. Why?
[4:17] That is because the BJT is a device that relies on both its "minority" and "majority" carriers. A "bipolar" device, hence the "B" in BJT.
[4:27] So in the case of an NPN BJT, the base is P-type, which means that it is full of electron holes.
[4:35] Turn it on, and electrons from the N-type emitter flow into that P-type base, where they are minority carriers.
[4:43] The base current also brings in electron holes, which are the majority carriers. The minority and majority carriers together make the base conductive - an effect known as "conductivity modulation".
[4:56] Conductivity modulation means that when the NPN BJT is on, there are electrons in the base where they do not belong. So to turn it off, we have to flush those minorities out ... wait that doesn't sound right ...
[5:09] Uh, well. Removing the injected minority carriers takes more time - which made them slower and somewhat challenging to use in things like electric motors, for instance.
[5:21] Third, BJTs lack the ruggedness to stay resilient under stress. They are prone to overheating. So
[5:29] engineers might need to add protection circuitry, which in turn adds undesired bulk.
[5:34] ## Power MOSFETs These multiple issues eventually led to Power MOSFETs in the late 1970s.
[5:40] The Power MOSFET is a MOSFET just like your logic CPU MOSFET - using the field effect to turn a non-conducting material into a conducting one. Good old MOSFETs.
[5:51] Unlike the BJT, it is a majority carrier device. It is unipolar. But the first MOSFETs were restricted to switching signals because they can only handle very low power levels, less than a watt.
[6:04] This changed with the introduction of new, vastly different structures like the Vertical Diffused MOS or Vertical MOS. Signal MOSFETs are lateral, meaning that the gate, source, and drain are all on the same plane.
[6:17] These vertical MOSFETs put the gate and source at the top of the bulk silicon, and the drain on the bottom.
[6:24] The Power MOSFET did a lot of the things that the Power Bipolar Transistor cannot. You can control it with voltage rather than a current. It switched much faster. And it was rugged.
[6:36] And in the late 1970s, power electronics people went back and forth as to whether it might replace the power BJT. In 1977, J.O.M. Jenkins of a company called
[6:46] Siliconix Limited published an article titled: "MOSPOWER- The Challenge to Power Bipolars".
[6:53] But the power MOSFET did have one major shortcoming: It does not handle high voltages as well as power BJTs can. So while it can be used for smaller devices like your personal computer,
[7:04] it was unsuitable for higher-power applications like an EV or a locomotive.
[7:09] ## Baliga In 1974, a young Indian-American researcher named B. Jayant Baliga joined General Electric's Corporate Research and Development Center.
[7:19] He was then assigned to work on power semiconductors. And at the start, he didn't want to do it. He thought power semiconductors were a 25-30 year old technology and a mature, unexciting field. But life makes you do a lot of things you don't want to do.
[7:34] He started working on thyristor technology. A year later in 1975, a Japanese professor named Jun-ichi Nishizawa announced a powerful new type of thyristor called the "static induction thyristor".
[7:47] General Electric thought it be prudent to make something similar, and assigned Baliga to the task. He worked on it for three or so years.
[7:54] And while he succeeded in producing some of these special thyristors, they exhibited default-ON behavior - which made them unsuitable for power applications.
[8:04] As Baliga struggled with this, he reflected upon the industry's ongoing back-and-forth debate between power BJTs and power MOSFETs. But as we discussed, both had their flaws.
[8:15] So that got him wondering. Can there be a voltage-controlled power semiconductor with the switching speed and resilience of a MOSFET, but capable of handling high power levels like a BJT can? Why not merge them together?
[8:28] ## MOSFET and BJT in the Telepod The thing that we now call the IGBT attempts to achieve the best of both worlds by combining aspects of the power MOSFET and power BJT.
[8:38] But freakier than that. Because the IGBT is not just a BJT and a MOSFET wired together. It's more like as if the two got mixed up inside the teleportation pods from that movie, the Fly.
[8:51] Imagine the IGBT as like having the strapping, muscular body of a vertically oriented BJT
[8:57] controlled by the power-efficient brain and spine of a MOSFET grafted onto it. It's semiconductor body horror.
[9:04] The IGBT has three terminals - a collector, emitter, and a gate. Note here how the thing has the MOSFET's gate and the BJT's emitter and collector. The collector is on the bottom.
[9:16] When the thing is off, no electrons can flow through from emitter to collector. And the thick, lightly doped, non-conducting drift region helps us block high voltages.
[9:27] Let us turn on our IGBT - which here in this example is an N-channel IGBT, which is more mainstream. As the gate starts receiving a voltage, electrons are attracted towards the body region right beneath the gate.
[9:41] This body region is P-type. Once the gate hits the threshold voltage, we repel enough electron holes
[9:49] and pull up enough electrons to create a conductive path across the surface of this P-type body region: The inversion layer. The inversion layer is the “MOSFET”
[9:59] part. It allows electrons to flow from the emitter through to the N-type drift region.
[10:05] And in doing so, we now activate the "bipolar" part of the IGBT action.
[10:11] This MOSFET electron flow now acts similarly to a current supplied to the base of a PNP BJT. So just like with a BJT, we get minority carrier injection.
[10:23] Electron holes are injected from a layer of P-type material right above the collector
[10:28] up into the N-type drift region, where they are minority carriers. In fact, many travel all the way up to the emitter metal. File that one away.
[10:39] Here, the combination of electrons and electron holes create the "conductivity modulation" effect that makes the power BJT so effective. Resistance greatly falls - allowing a big current to flow through.
[10:53] When we want to turn off the IGBT, we lower the voltage to the gate to below its threshold voltage - closing the flow of electrons from emitter to collector.
[11:02] Note that this IGBT will also suffer the same switch time issue as the BJT - removing the minority carriers from the drift area can take some time.
[11:12] ## GE and the IGT Baliga first documented his MOSFET-BJT hybrid idea in 1977 and wrote about it in September 1979 for Electronics Letters.
[11:24] Making it took different forces. This was General Electric during the time when that guy Jack Welch was making his presence known.
[11:31] A VP at GE came to the research team wanting a replacement for the power BJTs inside some

[11:50] Don't need to think too hard about what that means.
[11:53] So Baliga went back to this half-BJT, half-MOSFET idea and pitched it to the VP - pointing out that its applications stretched beyond simple variable speed motors to home appliances, lighting and many other GE businesses.
[12:08] The VP got very excited and told Welch himself. Welch fast-tracked the device in late 1980
[12:15] and the first wafers emerged in August 1981. Then Baliga first publicly presented the device at IEDM 1982 in December 1982, dubbing it the "Insulated Gate Rectifier".
[12:28] The product later hit the market in June 1983 as the "Insulated Gate Transistor", or IGT. The IGT was said to have won Electronic Products' Product of the Year award.
[12:39] ## RCA and the COMFET At around the same time however, two scientists at RCA were working on a similar idea.

[13:43] ## The Latch-up Issue Both devices however suffered a serious flaw: Latch-up.
[13:48] For those who were paying attention, you might have noticed that the thyristor and IGBT have common structures: They are both PNPN.
[13:56] And guys - YouTube says you are 98% men here - do you remember what was one of the big headaches of a Thyristor device? It latches, or has latch-up.
[14:06] The IGBT works like a MOSFET driving a PNP BJT. But every IGBT also has an unwanted, parasitic NPN BJT inside too.
[14:18] Going back to our original device example, holes are injected up from the P-type collector through the N-type drift and P-type body to the emitter metal contact.
[14:29] This sideways trip from the body to the metal runs into resistance, creating a voltage drop. If the current is strong enough and that voltage drop high enough, then that can accidentally trigger the hidden NPN bipolar transistor.
[14:43] This parasitic NPN BJT reacts with the normal PNP BJT to create a self-sustaining loop like
[14:50] that reactor at the end of the Spider-Man 2 movie. Closing the gate no longer shuts off the device, and the whole thing might threaten thermal runaway. Fire.
[15:01] Skeptics - including some of Baliga's very coworkers - flagged this problem from the very start. In response to latch-up, Baliga introduced something he called "Deep P+", a region of doped
[15:11] silicon inside the transistor that limits the flow of holes and makes latch-up harder to achieve.
[15:18] That being said, both GE's IGRs and IGTs and RCA's COMFETs can still suffer this behavior and it was up to the user to ensure that they stay within their operating windows.
[15:29] And to address the slow switching issue, Baliga introduced an innovative post-processing step to irradiate the silicon. This unusual-sounding step created lattice defects that trapped electrons after the IGBT gate turns off - speeding up its switching.
[15:45] Thanks to Baliga’s work, GE introduced a new adjustable speed motor for air conditioners - a new business. Moreover, the first IGRs off the
[15:54] line could be tailored from 60 hertz to 10 kilohertz to 100 kilohertz - fitting a series of applications ranging from motor drives to uninterruptible power supplies.
[16:04] ## Nakagawa When the IGR was first demonstrated in 1982, many Japanese companies were hesitant to produce their own versions.
[16:12] There was much skepticism. One such initial skeptic was a scientist at Toshiba named
[16:18] Akio Nakagawa. He attended the 1982 IEDM conference in the US, and apparently heard about Baliga's presentation.
[16:26] And initially, he wondered why anyone would care for a device that switches in more than 10 microseconds? But upon returning to Japan, he recognized that
[16:34] there existed many high-current applications that can tolerate its slower switching speeds.
[16:40] Some time later in early 1983, he was assigned to integrate a bipolar and MOSFET together onto one
[16:46] chip. He didn't seem to like that assignment and set out to create his own IGR or IGT, which means tackling the latch-up problem.
[16:55] To understand how he solved this, we need to understand two specific current limits: The first limit is the latch-up limit. This is when the voltage drop happens, the thyristor switches on, and then Otto can't shut down the reactor.
[17:09] The second is the saturation current limit. This is the maximum current that the IGBT can physically carry.
[17:16] Up until now, the approach that everyone was taking to prevent latch-up from happening was to raise the latch-up current limit.
[17:23] The problem is that the BJT’s saturation limit is high, making latch-up relatively easy to hit.
[17:30] Moreover, that latch-up limit is dynamic. It moves with certain factors - high temperatures via Joule heating being a significant one.
[17:38] So system architects always have to keep in the back of their mind that their IGBT can latch-up and undergo thermal runaway if they wandered out of this safe range. And frankly, that is a risk.
[17:49] Nakagawa's insight - which looks obvious in hindsight but is also why it is so brilliant - was to lower the device's saturation current to below its latch-up current limit. So
[17:59] the device will sooner top out than latch-up. It is outside the normal operating range.
[18:05] Toshiba started selling these new IGBTs in 1985. Now that their behavior is consistent
[18:11] across their whole operating range, they rapidly replaced power BJTs and MOSFETs in many medium and high voltage converter applications - remaining complementary to others.
[18:21] ## Credit So here comes the big question. Who invented this? This is not a patent office, but let us try to straighten this out.
[18:30] The basic concept of bipolar and MOSFETs mashed together has been around. They even had a name for it in Japan: Bi-FETs, for bipolar mode MOSFETs.
[18:39] In 1968, K. Yamagami of Mitsubishi filed for a Japanese patent for the idea of a PNP bipolar transistor driven by a MOSFET. It was granted a few years later.
[18:51] Ten years later in 1978, Jim Plummer and Scharf of Stanford University published work about a MOSFET controlling a special type of thyristor called a TRIAC.
[19:02] So the idea has been around, but ideas are cheap. Who brought it to fruition? The issue with figuring this is that Baliga at General Electric and Becke and Wheatley at RCA worked independently and their claims are nuanced.
[19:16] Baliga published his idea first (1979), and got an IGR to the market first (1982).
[19:23] He was also a true and fervent advocate of the device. He noted in an interview with Electronic Design that he pushed for the device to be used inside General Electric across their products.
[19:34] It should also be worth considering that Baliga continued working on IGBT throughout the early 1980s to solve its initial latching and slow-switching issues - filing over 20 patents in the meanwhile.
[19:46] He even wrote a big textbook: "The IGBT Device". The version I looked at is 800+ pages long. Becke and Wheatley by contrast seemed to move on to other things after their first patent.
[19:59] However, RCA does have their patent, which was quite early. And the key bit in their patent claim is that there is no latch-up behavior across the device's full operating range.
[20:09] Baliga's 1979 device mentions latch-up, saying that it has "thyristor-like" performance at
[20:15] higher currents. So the argument is that is not the IGBT as we know it and use today. The rebuttal to that though is that neither RCA nor GE had such a device at the time.
[20:29] In the end, it all comes down to what you think is the IGBT we are talking about. Is it the one that does or doesn’t latch-up across the whole operating range?
[20:39] Today most people seem willing to acknowledge that Baliga is the inventor, or at least the co-inventor. He has received - deservedly so - recognition and awards from the IEEE, the National Inventors Hall of Fame, etc.
[20:52] And Baliga himself has been gracious with credit in his oral histories and interviews. Nobody can deny his legacy.
[20:59] Everyone acknowledges that Nakagawa produced a non-latch-up IGBT and the major impact that device had in the industry.
[21:08] Though for his part, Nakagawa maintains a website attributing true invention to Becke. He believes that the real IGBT is the one that doesn’t latch up across its whole operating range.
[21:19] Such situations remind me of the question of who invented the integrated circuit. Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments made the original integrated circuit, but it wasn't quite the thing we know as the IC today.
[21:30] It took Fairchild's Bob Noyce and Jean Hoerni to come along with the planar method to make the practical monolithic integrated circuit. Then Fairchild and TI battled in courts for
[21:41] years to resolve the question. In the end, most people today just say that credit is shared.
